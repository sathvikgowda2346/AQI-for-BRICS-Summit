The gap

Official networks see the average city, not the actual air

Government monitoring stations are few, fixed, and expensive — built to characterize a city's baseline, not to catch a burning field forty kilometers upwind or a plume drifting across a national border overnight. By the time an official reading spikes, the exposure has usually already happened.

01 · Coverage

Sparse, stationary sensors

Most metro areas run on a handful of fixed monitors, leaving industrial fringes, informal settlements, and rural corridors effectively unmeasured.

02 · Borders

Smog doesn't respect jurisdiction

Agricultural burning and industrial plumes cross state and national lines, but monitoring, alerting, and response stay siloed within them.

03 · Timing

Detection lags exposure

Satellite passes and monthly reports confirm a hotspot existed — they rarely give authorities enough lead time to intervene before it peaks.

How it works

One pipeline, four kinds of evidence

01

Ingest

Every available signal, at whatever resolution it comes in.

  • Citizen photos with on-device haze scoring
  • Low-cost ground sensor networks
  • National satellite imagery (aerosol, NO₂, fire counts)
  • Meteorological wind and inversion data
02

Fuse

Align mismatched sources onto one spatial-temporal grid.

  • Calibrate citizen readings against nearby reference sensors
  • Resample satellite pixels to street-level corridors
  • Weight sources by confidence, not just recency
03

Detect & forecast

Find what stationary monitors would miss entirely.

  • Hotspot detection from sparse, noisy citizen input
  • 72-hour spike forecasts along economic corridors
  • Trans-boundary plume tracking using wind fields
04

Alert & coordinate

Get the finding to whoever can act on it, fast.

  • Tiered alerts to local, state, and national authorities
  • Cross-border handoff when a plume changes jurisdiction
  • Public advisories in local languages
The platform

Built for the people closest to the air

Hyper-local hotspot detection

A model trained to trust noisy, uneven citizen input — a handful of geotagged photos from one neighborhood is enough to flag a burn or a leak that no fixed monitor was close enough to catch.

Citizen reporting app

Snap a photo of visible haze or a flare stack; on-device scoring works even offline and syncs when connectivity returns.

Corridor forecasting

72-hour air quality outlooks for named economic corridors, not just city centers.

Authority alerting & handoff

Alerts route to the jurisdiction that can act — and automatically hand off to a neighboring authority when a plume is forecast to cross a border, with the evidence trail attached.

Satellite fusion

Aerosol, NO₂, and active-fire layers resampled onto the same grid as ground and citizen data.

Offline-first design

Built for low-connectivity regions where the pollution is worst and the infrastructure is thinnest.

Interoperability

Nations share the model, not the raw data

Each BRICS nation runs Aeromesh on its own infrastructure, over its own citizen and sensor data. What crosses the border is the trained model — not the underlying photos, sensor logs, or personal data behind it.

A
Local training, shared weightsEvery country fine-tunes the hotspot and forecast models on local conditions, then contributes only model updates to the shared federation.
B
Common alert schemaA shared data format means a hotspot flagged in one country is legible to the authority across the border, with no manual translation of formats.
C
Sovereignty by defaultEach national instance can withhold, delay, or restrict what it federates — participation is additive, never a requirement to expose raw data.
